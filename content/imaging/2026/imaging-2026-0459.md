## 문제
A 71-year-old man comes to the emergency department 2 hours after sudden, painless loss of vision in his right eye while reading the newspaper. He has hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and a 40-pack-year smoking history. He has no headache, jaw pain, or scalp tenderness. Visual acuity is counting fingers in the right eye and 20/25 in the left eye. There is a right relative afferent pupillary defect. Intraocular pressure is normal in both eyes. Fundoscopic examination of the right eye shows a pale, whitened posterior retina with a bright red spot at the fovea and narrowed, segmented arterioles; there are no retinal hemorrhages. A carotid bruit is heard on the right. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

- A. Vitreous hemorrhage
- B. Nonarteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy
- C. Central retinal artery occlusion
- D. Central retinal vein occlusion
- E. Rhegmatogenous retinal detachment

## 정답 및 해설
> 정답: C

- **정답 핵심**: Sudden painless monocular vision loss with a relative afferent pupillary defect, a pale edematous retina, a cherry-red spot at the fovea, and segmented ('boxcarring') arterioles is central retinal artery occlusion. The ipsilateral carotid bruit and vascular risk factors suggest an embolus from carotid atherosclerosis; absence of headache and jaw claudication makes giant cell arteritis less likely.
- **원리**: <b>Why the retina turns white and the fovea red</b>: the inner retina is supplied by the central retinal artery. When it is occluded, the inner retinal layers become ischemic and edematous, and the swollen ganglion-cell layer looks opaque white. The fovea has no inner retinal layers, so the normal choroidal circulation underneath still shows through — the "cherry-red spot."<br> <b>Why it is a stroke equivalent</b>: most nonarteritic CRAO is embolic (carotid plaque, cardiac source), and the risk of cerebral stroke is highest in the first weeks. Patients need urgent stroke-pathway evaluation: carotid imaging, cardiac rhythm monitoring, and echocardiography.<br> <b>Always consider giant cell arteritis</b> in patients over 50: headache, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, and high ESR/CRP call for immediate glucocorticoids to protect the other eye.
- **비교**: <table><thead><tr><th style="width:24%">Feature</th><th style="width:38%">Retinal artery occlusion (answer)</th><th>Retinal vein occlusion (closest distractor)</th></tr></thead><tbody> <tr><td>Retina</td><td><b>Pale, white, edematous</b></td><td>Diffuse "blood and thunder" hemorrhages</td></tr> <tr><td>Fovea</td><td>Cherry-red spot</td><td>Macular edema</td></tr> <tr><td>Vessels</td><td>Narrow, segmented arterioles</td><td>Dilated, tortuous veins; disc swelling</td></tr> <tr><td>Onset</td><td>Abrupt, profound loss</td><td>Sudden or subacute, often milder</td></tr> </tbody></table> Both are painless and monocular — the color of the retina (white vs. hemorrhagic) decides.
- **오답 이유**:
  - (A) Vitreous hemorrhage is correct when the red reflex is lost and the fundus cannot be seen, typically in proliferative diabetic retinopathy.
  - (B) Nonarteritic ischemic optic neuropathy is correct when the patient wakes with vision loss and has a swollen optic disc with a normal-appearing retina.
  - (D) Central retinal vein occlusion is correct when the fundus shows widespread retinal hemorrhages, dilated tortuous veins, and disc swelling rather than a pale retina.
  - (E) Retinal detachment is correct when flashes, floaters, and a curtain over the visual field precede the loss and a gray elevated retina is seen.
- **함정**: Choosing vein occlusion because both are sudden and painless — a pale retina with a cherry-red spot means arterial occlusion, not hemorrhage.
- **학습목표**: 갑작스러운 통증 없는 한쪽 시력 소실, 상대적 구심동공운동장애, 창백한 망막과 체리붉은점에서 망막중심동맥폐쇄를 진단한다
