## 문제
A 20-month-old boy is brought to the emergency department 1 hour after a seizure at home. His mother says he had a fever since the morning and then his whole body stiffened and jerked for about 2 minutes; afterward he was sleepy for 20 minutes. This is his first seizure. He is fully immunized, including Haemophilus influenzae type b and pneumococcal vaccines, and has not received antibiotics. His development is normal. His father had a similar seizure as a toddler. His temperature is 39.4°C (102.9°F). He is now alert, playful, and has clear rhinorrhea. His neck is supple, and the anterior fontanelle is closed. Neurologic examination shows no focal findings. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

- A. Daily oral levetiracetam prophylaxis
- B. Reassurance and parental education
- C. Lumbar puncture for CSF analysis
- D. Electroencephalography within 24 hours
- E. MRI of the brain with contrast

## 정답 및 해설
> 정답: B

- **정답 핵심**: A generalized seizure lasting less than 15 minutes, occurring once in 24 hours with fever, in a developmentally normal child aged 6 months to 5 years who has returned to baseline, is a simple febrile seizure. A family history is common. With a supple neck, full immunization, no prior antibiotics, and a normal examination, meningitis is unlikely and lumbar puncture is not routinely needed. EEG, neuroimaging, and anticonvulsants do not change the prognosis; the appropriate step is reassurance and education.
- **원리**: <b>Why young children seize with fever</b>: Between 6 months and 5 years, the brain's excitatory networks mature faster than its inhibitory GABA circuits, and the seizure threshold is temperature-sensitive. A rapid rise in temperature, often from a viral illness, can trigger a brief generalized seizure. Genetic susceptibility is strong — a first-degree relative raises the risk.<br> <b>Why tests add nothing in the simple form</b>: A simple febrile seizure is a provoked event in a normal brain. EEG abnormalities after it do not predict epilepsy, imaging is normal, and the risk of later epilepsy (about 1%) is barely above that of the general population. Daily anticonvulsants reduce recurrence slightly but do not prevent epilepsy, and their side effects outweigh this benefit.<br> <b>When to look harder</b>: Features of a complex febrile seizure (focal, longer than 15 minutes, or recurrent within 24 hours) or signs of meningitis (meningismus, persistent altered consciousness, incomplete immunization, or pretreatment with antibiotics that can mask signs) shift the balance toward lumbar puncture or imaging.

- **오답 이유**:
  - (A) Daily levetiracetam would be correct only for recurrent unprovoked seizures that meet the definition of epilepsy, not for simple febrile seizures.
  - (C) Lumbar puncture would be correct if the child had meningismus, did not return to baseline, was incompletely immunized, or had been pretreated with antibiotics.
  - (D) Electroencephalography would be correct if the seizure were afebrile or focal, or if there were developmental regression suggesting an epilepsy syndrome.
  - (E) MRI of the brain would be correct if there were a focal neurologic deficit, a focal seizure, macrocephaly, or signs of increased intracranial pressure.
- **함정**: Performing a lumbar puncture because fever and seizure occurred together — in a fully immunized toddler back to baseline with a supple neck, a simple febrile seizure needs only reassurance.
