## 문제
A 4-week-old boy is brought to the physician because of vomiting after every feeding for 5 days. The vomiting has become forceful, and the vomitus is nonbilious formula. He is hungry immediately after vomiting. He was born at term after an uncomplicated pregnancy and is the family's first child. He has had fewer wet diapers today. His weight is 140 g less than at his visit 1 week ago. The mucous membranes are dry. A firm, mobile, olive-sized mass is palpable in the epigastrium, and visible gastric peristaltic waves move from left to right across the upper abdomen. Which of the following sets of serum findings is most likely in this patient?

- A. Normal chloride, normal potassium, normal bicarbonate
- B. Low chloride, low potassium, high bicarbonate
- C. High chloride, low potassium, low bicarbonate
- D. Normal chloride, high potassium, low bicarbonate
- E. Low chloride, high potassium, high bicarbonate

## 정답 및 해설
> 정답: B

- **정답 핵심**: A 4-week-old first-born boy with projectile nonbilious vomiting, hunger after vomiting, an olive-shaped epigastric mass, and visible peristalsis has hypertrophic pyloric stenosis. Repeated loss of gastric hydrochloric acid causes hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis, and volume depletion with renal compensation causes potassium loss — low chloride, low potassium, high bicarbonate.
- **원리**: <b>Why alkalosis</b>: Each liter of gastric juice carries hydrogen and chloride. Every H+ secreted into the stomach leaves one bicarbonate in the blood; normally the bicarbonate is neutralized when acid reaches the duodenum. With the pylorus closed, the acid is vomited, so bicarbonate accumulates and chloride falls.<br> <b>Why potassium falls</b>: Vomiting causes volume loss, which activates the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system. Aldosterone makes the distal nephron reabsorb sodium in exchange for potassium and hydrogen. Alkalosis also shifts potassium into cells.<br> <b>Paradoxical aciduria</b>: When potassium is depleted, the kidney keeps sodium by excreting H+, so urine becomes acidic despite systemic alkalosis. Correcting volume, chloride, and potassium with saline plus potassium <b>before</b> pyloromyotomy matters, because alkalosis depresses respiratory drive and raises the risk of postoperative apnea.
- **비교**: <table><thead><tr><th style="width:24%">Feature</th><th style="width:38%">Pyloric stenosis vomiting (answer)</th><th>Diarrhea in an infant (closest distractor pattern)</th></tr></thead><tbody> <tr><td>Fluid lost</td><td>Gastric HCl</td><td>Bicarbonate-rich intestinal fluid</td></tr> <tr><td>Chloride</td><td><b>Low</b></td><td>High (normal anion gap acidosis)</td></tr> <tr><td>Bicarbonate</td><td><b>High</b></td><td>Low</td></tr> <tr><td>Potassium</td><td>Low</td><td>Low</td></tr> </tbody></table> Both lose potassium — chloride and bicarbonate move in opposite directions depending on whether acid or base is lost.
- **오답 이유**:
  - (A) Normal values would be expected only very early in the illness, before repeated vomiting and dehydration — not after 5 days with weight loss.
  - (C) High chloride, low potassium, and low bicarbonate would be correct for infant diarrhea, which loses bicarbonate and causes non-anion gap acidosis.
  - (D) Normal chloride, high potassium, and low bicarbonate would fit salt-wasting congenital adrenal hyperplasia, which also causes vomiting in infants of this age.
  - (E) Low chloride with high potassium and high bicarbonate mixes patterns; high potassium would suggest adrenal insufficiency, which causes acidosis rather than alkalosis.
- **함정**: Confusing pyloric stenosis with salt-wasting adrenal hyperplasia, which also vomits at 2–4 weeks but has high potassium and acidosis.
