## 문제
A 64-year-old woman comes to the physician for a follow-up visit. She was diagnosed with essential thrombocythemia 1 year ago and takes low-dose aspirin. She feels well and has no muscle weakness or palpitations. She takes no potassium supplements, ACE inhibitors, or diuretics. Pulse is 76/min and regular, and blood pressure is 128/78 mm Hg. Laboratory studies show a hemoglobin of 13.1 g/dL, leukocyte count of 8,200/mm3, platelet count of 1,150,000/mm3, serum sodium of 140 mEq/L, serum potassium of 6.4 mEq/L, and serum creatinine of 0.8 mg/dL. The laboratory reports that the specimen was not hemolyzed. An ECG shows normal sinus rhythm with normal T waves. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

- A. Start oral patiromer and recheck potassium in 1 week
- B. Measure the transtubular potassium gradient in urine
- C. Measure plasma renin activity and aldosterone
- D. Measure potassium in a heparinized plasma sample
- E. Administer intravenous calcium gluconate

## 정답 및 해설
> 정답: D

- **정답 핵심**: Serum potassium is high, but nothing supports true hyperkalemia: she is asymptomatic, the ECG has normal T waves, renal function is normal, and she takes no drug that raises potassium. Her platelet count is over 1 million/mm3. When blood clots in a serum tube, activated platelets release their intracellular potassium, so serum potassium rises in proportion to the platelet count while the patient's plasma potassium is normal — pseudohyperkalemia. Hemolysis was excluded. The next step is to measure potassium in an unclotted (heparinized) plasma sample, ideally analyzed promptly; a plasma value that is normal and more than about 0.4 mEq/L lower than serum confirms it.
- **원리**: <b>Why serum and plasma differ</b>: serum is what remains after blood clots; plasma is the fluid of anticoagulated blood. During clotting, platelets are activated and release granule contents and cytosolic potassium. Normally this adds only about 0.1–0.4 mEq/L, but with platelet counts above roughly 500,000–1,000,000/mm3 (or extreme leukocytosis) the added potassium becomes large enough to look like hyperkalemia.<br> <b>Why the ECG and the patient matter</b>: true hyperkalemia lowers the resting membrane potential toward threshold, causing peaked T waves, conduction slowing, and weakness. A potassium of 6.4 mEq/L with a normal ECG and no reason to retain potassium should make the clinician question the measurement before treating it.<br> <b>Why treating would harm</b>: potassium binders or insulin given for a spurious value can cause real hypokalemia. The fix is in the specimen, not in the patient.

- **오답 이유**:
  - (A) Patiromer would be correct for confirmed chronic hyperkalemia, for example in chronic kidney disease on an ACE inhibitor, after a plasma value confirms it.
  - (B) The urine potassium gradient would be correct once true hyperkalemia is confirmed and the question is whether renal excretion is inappropriately low.
  - (C) Renin and aldosterone would be correct for confirmed hyperkalemia with low renal potassium excretion, to look for hypoaldosteronism or type 4 RTA.
  - (E) IV calcium gluconate would be correct if the ECG showed peaked T waves or QRS widening from confirmed hyperkalemia, to stabilize the myocardium.
- **함정**: Treating the number — a serum potassium of 6.4 with a normal ECG and a platelet count above 1 million is pseudohyperkalemia until proven otherwise.
- **학습목표**: 혈소판이 매우 많은 무증상 환자의 혈청 칼륨 상승을 응고 중 혈소판의 칼륨 방출에 의한 가성고칼륨혈증으로 의심하고 헤파린 혈장 칼륨으로 확인한다
