## 문제
A 74-year-old man with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction comes to the physician because of worsening shortness of breath for 2 weeks. Three days ago, his furosemide dose was doubled, and his dyspnea has improved. He has no fever, cough, chest pain, or weight loss. Temperature is 36.8°C, pulse is 84/min, and blood pressure is 118/72 mm Hg. There are decreased breath sounds and dullness at both lung bases, more on the right. Chest x-ray shows cardiomegaly and bilateral pleural effusions. Thoracentesis on the right yields straw-colored fluid. Results: pleural fluid protein 3.7 g/dL (serum 6.1 g/dL); pleural fluid LDH 150 U/L (serum 210 U/L; upper limit of normal 220 U/L); pleural fluid albumin 1.6 g/dL (serum 3.3 g/dL); pleural fluid nucleated cells 600/μL, 80% lymphocytes; glucose 110 mg/dL; Gram stain negative. Which of the following is the most likely cause of the effusion?

- A. Malignant pleural effusion
- B. Tuberculous pleuritis
- C. Pulmonary embolism
- D. Heart failure
- E. Parapneumonic effusion

## 정답 및 해설
> 정답: D

- **정답 핵심**: By Light's criteria the fluid is an exudate only because the pleural-to-serum protein ratio is 0.61 (above 0.5); the LDH ratio is 0.71, but the pleural LDH (150 U/L) is below two-thirds of the upper limit of normal. Diuresis removes water from the pleural space faster than protein and concentrates the fluid. In a patient with heart failure on recently increased diuretics, a serum-to-pleural albumin gradient greater than 1.2 g/dL (here 1.7 g/dL) indicates a transudate. Bilateral effusions, cardiomegaly, improvement with diuretics, and bland fluid all point to heart failure.
- **원리**: <b>Why Light's criteria misfire here</b>: Light's criteria were designed to be sensitive for exudates — an effusion is called an exudate if any one of three criteria is met (protein ratio above 0.5, LDH ratio above 0.6, or pleural LDH above two-thirds of the upper limit of normal). The price is that about a quarter of heart-failure effusions are misclassified as exudates, especially after diuresis, which removes water from the pleural space and concentrates protein and LDH.<br> <b>Why the albumin gradient helps</b>: In a transudate, the pleural fluid is an ultrafiltrate formed by increased hydrostatic pressure, so albumin stays largely in the plasma and the serum-minus-pleural difference is large. When it exceeds 1.2 g/dL, the effusion behaves like a transudate even if Light's criteria are borderline. A high pleural fluid NT-proBNP gives the same answer.<br> <b>What this spares the patient</b>: Recognizing a pseudoexudate avoids an unnecessary search for cancer or tuberculosis (pleural biopsy, CT) in a patient whose effusion will resolve with treatment of heart failure.

- **오답 이유**:
  - (A) Malignant pleural effusion would be correct with weight loss, a unilateral large effusion, a serum-pleural albumin gradient below 1.2 g/dL, and positive cytology.
  - (B) Tuberculous pleuritis would be correct for a unilateral lymphocytic exudate with high adenosine deaminase, fever, and night sweats in a patient with exposure risk.
  - (C) Pulmonary embolism would be correct with sudden pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia, hypoxemia, and a small, often bloody exudative effusion.
  - (E) Parapneumonic effusion would be correct with fever, cough, a pulmonary infiltrate, and neutrophil-predominant fluid, often with low glucose and pH.
- **함정**: Stopping at Light's criteria — after diuresis a heart-failure effusion can meet one exudate criterion and lead to an unnecessary cancer or TB work-up.
