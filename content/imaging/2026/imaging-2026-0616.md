## 문제
A 22-year-old woman with type 1 diabetes mellitus is brought to the emergency department because of nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain for 2 days. She ran out of insulin 4 days ago. Her pulse is 118/min, respirations are 30/min and deep, and blood pressure is 98/62 mm Hg. Mucous membranes are dry. Laboratory studies show a glucose of 520 mg/dL, sodium 132 mEq/L, potassium 3.0 mEq/L, chloride 96 mEq/L, bicarbonate 8 mEq/L, and creatinine 1.3 mg/dL; arterial pH is 7.10, and serum β-hydroxybutyrate is markedly elevated. An infusion of 0.9% saline is started at 1 L/h. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

- A. Subcutaneous insulin glargine
- B. Intravenous 5% dextrose in water
- C. Intravenous potassium chloride before starting insulin
- D. Intravenous regular insulin infusion at 0.1 U/kg/h immediately
- E. Intravenous sodium bicarbonate infusion

## 정답 및 해설
> 정답: C

- **정답 핵심**: She has diabetic ketoacidosis: glucose 520 mg/dL, pH 7.10, bicarbonate 8 mEq/L, an anion gap of 132 − (96 + 8) = 28, and elevated β-hydroxybutyrate after stopping insulin. Fluids have been started. Her serum potassium is 3.0 mEq/L despite acidosis, which means total body potassium is severely depleted. Insulin drives potassium into cells and would cause dangerous hypokalemia and arrhythmia, so when potassium is below 3.3 mEq/L, insulin is held and potassium is replaced intravenously (about 20-30 mEq/h) until it rises above 3.3 mEq/L.
- **원리**: <b>Why a "normal or low" potassium in DKA is alarming</b>: In DKA, insulin deficiency and hyperosmolality shift potassium out of cells, and acidosis contributes to the shift. At the same time, osmotic diuresis and vomiting waste large amounts of potassium in urine and gastric fluid. Total body deficits are typically 3-5 mEq/kg, yet the serum level is usually normal or high at presentation. A low serum level therefore reveals an even larger deficit.<br> <b>What insulin does next</b>: Insulin activates the Na+/K+-ATPase and moves potassium back into cells within minutes; correcting acidosis adds to the shift. Starting insulin at a potassium of 3.0 can drop it below 2.5, causing respiratory muscle weakness and ventricular arrhythmias.<br> <b>The sequence</b>: Fluids first, then check potassium: below 3.3 → replace potassium and delay insulin; 3.3-5.2 → give insulin and add potassium to fluids; above 5.2 → insulin without potassium, recheck every 2 hours.
- **비교**: <table><thead><tr><th style="width:24%">Serum potassium</th><th style="width:38%">Below 3.3 mEq/L (this patient)</th><th>3.3-5.2 mEq/L (closest distractor scenario)</th></tr></thead><tbody> <tr><td>Insulin</td><td><b>Hold</b> until potassium is above 3.3</td><td>Start 0.1 U/kg/h infusion</td></tr> <tr><td>Potassium</td><td>20-30 mEq/h IV</td><td>20-30 mEq per liter of fluid</td></tr> <tr><td>Main danger</td><td>Severe hypokalemia, arrhythmia, respiratory muscle weakness</td><td>Ongoing ketosis if insulin is delayed</td></tr> </tbody></table> The serum potassium decides the order: insulin is the cornerstone of DKA treatment, but only after potassium is high enough to absorb the intracellular shift.
- **오답 이유**:
  - (A) Subcutaneous glargine is given when ketoacidosis has resolved and the patient can eat, overlapping 2 hours with the infusion, not as initial therapy.
  - (B) Dextrose is added once glucose falls to about 200-250 mg/dL so insulin can continue clearing ketones, not at a glucose of 520 mg/dL.
  - (D) An immediate insulin infusion would be correct if the serum potassium were 3.3 mEq/L or higher, with potassium added to the fluids.
  - (E) Sodium bicarbonate is considered only when pH is below 6.9, and it would further lower potassium by driving it into cells.
- **함정**: Starting insulin right away because the glucose is high — with potassium below 3.3 mEq/L, insulin must wait until potassium is replaced.
- **학습목표**: 혈청 칼륨 3.3 mEq/L 미만인 당뇨병케톤산증에서 인슐린보다 칼륨 보충을 먼저 고르고 즉시 인슐린 투여와 가른다
