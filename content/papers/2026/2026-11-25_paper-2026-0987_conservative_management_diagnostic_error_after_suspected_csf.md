## Title
Conservative management: diagnostic error after suspected CSF leak

## Authors
Gross Sarah A

## Journal / DOI
Diagnosis (Berlin, Germany) · DOI: 10.1515/dx-2026-0166 · PMID: 43077598
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43077598/

## Abstract
**OBJECTIVES:** To trace how a suspected but unexamined surgical complication propagated through non-disclosure, anchoring, and fragmented care into months of diagnostic delay, unresolved illness, and lasting harm.

**CASE PRESENTATION:** A 23-year-old woman developed persistent clear nasal drainage after elective functional endoscopic sinus surgery and asked her surgeon whether it was a cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak. Beta-2 transferrin was never ordered. The complication was never confirmed or excluded. In March of her first year of medical school, at 24, she became acutely ill. An early lumbar puncture was reported to her as viral meningitis and not investigated further. That record is lost, and the only surviving lumbar puncture was essentially normal. Over many months she cycled through various emergency departments, dismissed as drug-seeking and a frequent flier, then was committed to a psychiatric unit. She had seizures before receiving intravenous treatment for presumed Lyme meningitis and for toxoplasmosis supported by elevated Toxoplasma IgM and IgG. Medical school ended. Cognitive impairment and fatigue persist 20 years later.

**CONCLUSIONS:** The failure was not one missed clue but a cascade in which each error made the next more likely: a suspected leak became an incomplete history, then a confident but incorrect psychiatric closure. Two mechanisms stand out: a private workup withheld from the treating team, and an illness that corrupted the patient's symptom reporting and generated false histories clinicians anchored upon, even as her core account, that her illness was organic, stayed correct. Disclosure, ownership of the whole picture, and repeated reassessment can interrupt such a cascade.
